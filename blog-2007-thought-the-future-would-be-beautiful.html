2007 thought the future would be beautiful

2007 thought the future would be beautiful

Ian McCallum · August 26, 2026 · 6 min read

Look at this website for a second. The glass, the glossy blue, the taskbar along the bottom, the wallpaper that looks like a screensaver from a middle school computer lab. I built it to look like Windows Vista on purpose, and most people assume it is a joke.

It is not a joke. It is the closest thing I have to a thesis.

What that look actually was

The design language is called Frutiger Aero. Roughly 2004 to 2013. Water, grass, sky, bubbles, fish, and a great deal of very shiny glass. Vista shipped with a green hill under a blue sky. Everything had a reflection. Everything looked slightly wet.

Strip the gradients off and here is what it was actually saying: the future is going to be great. Clean, bright, connected, alive. Technology and nature were going to get along fine. That assumption was baked into the pixels, and nobody had to argue for it, because everybody felt it.

Then it went away. Design went flat, then gray, then black. And somewhere in the same stretch the stories we tell about technology flipped over. The future stopped being a place we were excited to get to and became a thing to brace for.

I am 18, and I inherited the second version

I do not really remember the optimistic era. I was born into the tail end of it. I got Frutiger Aero the way you get music your parents played, secondhand and slightly out of context, which is probably why I like it as much as I do.

What I got firsthand is this: I started building with AI at the exact moment when the loudest story about AI is fear. Job loss. Slop. Extinction, on the more energetic days.

I do not think those conversations are stupid. Some of them are the most important ones happening right now. But I have noticed that "the future is terrifying" and "the future is wonderful" share a problem. Neither one tells you what to build on Monday.

Optimism has to have a target

Here is the part I actually believe, and it is narrower than it probably sounds.

I am an optimist about AI. But optimism about a technology is worth nothing unless you say who it is optimism for. "AI will change everything" is not a belief. It is a weather report.

So: change things for whom?

Look at where the effort is going. The demos are people writing code, summarizing meetings, building slide decks, generating images. Almost all of it points at people who already have leverage. Engineers. Knowledge workers. Companies with an IT budget and somebody whose actual job is to evaluate tools.

The most useful thing you can do with a powerful new technology is carry it to the people who do not have time to come get it.

The people who are not in the demo

There are millions of Americans running small trades businesses. HVAC, plumbing, electrical, roofing. A guy with two or three trucks.

His phone rings while he is under a sink with both hands full. He does not answer. The customer does not leave a voicemail, because nobody leaves voicemails anymore. She calls the next result on Google.

That call was worth a few hundred dollars, or eight thousand if the furnace is dead. He will never know it happened. There is no notification for a job you never heard about. It just quietly does not exist, over and over, for years.

That is not a glamorous problem. There is no demo video in it. It is also enormous, it is happening right now, and it is happening to people who work harder than almost anyone I know.

What I am building

I run a company called Beat the Clock, and the product is called Clockwork. It catches the call he missed and texts the customer back in seconds. It drafts the reply in his voice. It prices the quote off his own rate card instead of a guess. It chases the follow-up he was never going to get around to, and it collects the invoice.

He approves the messages. It works for him, not around him.

The hard part was not the part I expected

Making it smart was not the hard part. Making it honest was.

Most AI products are built so that there is always an answer. That is the default behavior, and in a chat window it is mostly harmless. Inside somebody's business it is a disaster. If my software invents a price, promises a Thursday his calendar cannot cover, or claims it made him money it cannot trace to a real job, then it did not save him any time. It started a small fire in his name.

So a lot of the build went into teaching it to say nothing. It is not allowed to cite a number it cannot point at. It is not allowed to promise capacity it cannot see. When it does not know, it says so, and a person decides.

A contractor who gets burned once by software that lied to his customer in his name will never trust software again, and he would be right not to. Trust is not a feature of the product. Trust is the product.

What I want to be true in ten years

  • I want the gap between when a technology exists and when it reaches a two truck shop to be measured in months, not decades.
  • I want AI to stop being a product category and go back to being plumbing. Nobody sells you a website with electricity in it. It is just in there, doing its job, and no one says the word.
  • I want the person who fixes your furnace to run software as good as anything a startup in San Francisco uses, at a price that is not insulting.
  • And I want to be wrong about the frightening parts. If I am, I would rather have spent this decade building the version that is not frightening than writing about the version that is.

Where I actually am

I am 18. I just moved to Champaign to start at UIUC studying finance and data science. I have one product, no famous customers, and a fairly long list of things I have already been wrong about.

So take this for what it is. Not a prediction. A direction. I am pointing my optimism at people with trucks.

And I am keeping the website like this. The future should look like somebody was looking forward to it.

If you run a trades business and any of this sounded like your Tuesday, I would genuinely like to hear about it. You can reach me through this site, or see what I am building at beatyourclock.com.
